Q30. [Historical Source Extract — Chapter 1: The Socratic Dialogue on Justice]

Read the following adapted dialogue from Book I of Plato’s The Republic and answer the questions that follow:

Socrates: “Come now, Thrasymachus, tell us what you assert justice to be. Do you claim that it is the advantage of the stronger?”
Thrasymachus: “Indeed, I do! In every city, the government lays down laws that are advantageous to itself: a democracy makes democratic laws, a tyranny makes tyrannical laws, and so on. In making these laws, they declare that what is advantageous for them is just for their subjects; and whoever transgresses it, they punish as a lawbreaker and an unjust man. This, my good Socrates, is what I mean: in all cities, the same thing is just—namely, the advantage of the established ruling power.”
Socrates: “I must first learn what you mean; for as yet, I do not understand. You say that what is advantageous to the stronger is just. But surely, Thrasymachus, the true ruler or the true physician does not seek their own advantage, but the good of the subject or the patient committed to their care…”

Sub-Questions:

  1. State the cynical definition of “Justice” advanced by the Sophist philosopher Thrasymachus in this dialogue. [1 Mark]
  2. What analogy does Socrates employ to refute Thrasymachus’ claim that rulers act solely for their personal, self-interested advantage? [1 Mark]
  3. Analyze how this dialogue between Socrates and Thrasymachus illustrates the true essence and methodology of Political Theory as a discipline of rational interrogation rather than passive acceptance of power. [2 Marks]
